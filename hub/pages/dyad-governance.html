Collective Governance Model

How dyad members become active co-designers of the community — and where the startup keeps the wheel. A decision-rights partition informed by Inclusive Organizing, Subvert, and Blacksky.

v0.2 — 14 July 2026 · status: draft for discussion · full document suite: dyad Governance Documents v0.1

Premise

The projects that make member co-design work don't ask "should members decide things?" They partition decision domains explicitly and write it down. Subvert codifies everything in bylaws. Blacksky separates the infrastructure it provides from the rules each community sets. Inclusive Organizing supplies the method: consent-based decisions, versioned natural-language agreements, minimum viable governance.

Members co-design within defined domains with real decision power — not advisory input on everything. The line between domains is drawn publicly, so it never feels arbitrary.

Three layers
Startup decides

Strategic

Existential and fiduciary decisions. Members are informed with reasoning — never surprised, never voting.

  • Fundraise, equity, legal entity
  • Hiring & runway
  • Pricing & business model
  • Pivots, brand, funded partnerships

Obligation: quarterly “State of dyad” — what was decided and why.

Members decide

Tactical

The co-design domain. Decisions pass by consent of members — this is the answer to “on what?”

  • Community standards & moderation policy
  • Membership criteria & onboarding
  • Programming & curation (events, /voices)
  • Community budget allocation

Backstop: startup retains legal/safety removals only.

Role holder decides

Operational

Whoever holds the role acts — no group decision needed. Consent applies to policies, not actions.

  • Running an event within the agreed program
  • Moderating per the agreed standards
  • Writing field notes & voices
  • Welcoming new members

Guard: if every action needs a group, we've rebuilt bureaucracy.

Mechanics
(from IO)

Consent, not consensus, not voting

A proposal in a tactical domain passes unless someone raises a reasoned objection — “this causes harm” or “this breaks an agreement” — not a preference. Fast, and it structurally guarantees quiet members are heard: objections must be resolved, but nobody can filibuster with taste.

Versioned, natural-language agreements

dyad Community Agreements v0.1 — one page, plain language, public, with a changelog. Anyone proposes an amendment → 72-hour consent round → merged or resolved. This is the community-standards process and the co-design process in one mechanism. Every rule is revocable the same way it was made.

Shared language

A ten-term glossary — member, steward, proposal, objection, agreement — defined before onboarding begins. Most governance fights are definitional; this prevents them.

Minimum viable governance

No councils, boards, or working groups on day one. Start with the agreements doc, the proposal process, and the budget pilot. Add a working group only when a domain has recurring decisions and volunteers — standards & moderation will likely be first.

Mechanics
(from Subvert's docs)

Read directly from Subvert's published Community Standards, Member Agreement, Acceptance Criteria, and Bylaws — five mechanisms dyad can adopt without being a co-op:

Two-document architecture

Subvert splits its rules into Bylaws (the constitution — only members can amend it) and Member Agreements (practical details — the board updates them with notice, and anyone who disagrees may exit within 30 days). For dyad: a short Charter holding the decision-rights partition itself (changed only by member consent) and the Community Agreements (evolving via the lighter 72-hour process). Changing the rules is harder than changing a rule.

Plain-language explainers beside the formal text

Every dense clause in Subvert's legal documents gets a green box saying what it means in ordinary words, explicitly marked non-binding. Adopt this format for anything dyad publishes that has legal or procedural weight.

The enforcement ladder

Notice and education first; no unilateral removal — “no member will be permanently removed through the unilateral decision of a single administrator”; final removal requires democratic review by a cross-section of members; the accused has a right to be heard before the decision. The only carve-out is legal and safety removals. This turns dyad's “member expulsion” row from a vague promise into a concrete procedure.

Good standing, with automatic reinstatement

Subvert's activity bar is deliberately minimal (log in once a year, keep one release up). Inactive members lose their vote but are reinstated automatically by simply engaging again — no re-application. For dyad this solves the consent-round dead-weight problem: quorum is counted against active members, and drifting away is never punished.

The petition valve

Subvert members have an escalation path that bypasses leadership entirely: 10% of members can petition a bylaws amendment onto the agenda with no board sign-off. dyad's equivalent: some threshold of active members (say 20% while small) can force any topic into a consent round even if the founders disagree. This is the anti-capture guarantee that makes “startup decides strategic” trustworthy.

Taste-free membership criteria

Applications are judged by compensated member-curators against published criteria “with no reference to personal taste or judgement of artistic merit, nor to the size of an artist's audience.” Rejections come with guidance; a second rejection unlocks an appeal; reapplying is always allowed. A template for dyad's membership-criteria domain: objective rubric, member reviewers, a ladder instead of a wall.

Reference models

Inclusive Organizing

Open framework · Greg Cassel

Toolbox for consent-based, non-hierarchical organizing. Low-tech, reversible, natural-language agreements; a shared vocabulary (MOO).

We take: the method — consent rounds, versioned agreements, shared language, minimum viable governance.

Subvert

Music marketplace co-op

10,000+ member co-op holding voting control over a public-benefit corporation that takes investment. One member, one vote; codified bylaws; transparent finances; elected board.

We take: codify everything and publish it; equal voice within the community domain; the dual-entity structure as a future path if members ever hold real ownership.

Blacksky

Community infrastructure on AT Protocol

Communities set their own rules and moderate together; the platform provides tools, not rulings. Pooled funds allocated democratically — servers, creators, local projects.

We take: community-set moderation with a startup safety backstop; a real pooled budget that makes co-design material, not advisory.

First steps
  1. Ratify the document suite — drafted hereCharter, Community Standards, Membership Criteria, Member Agreement, Governance Roadmap, Glossary. Ratification by the founding cohort is the first consent round: “this is v0.1 — propose changes.”
  2. Set up the proposal channel and decision logA Notion table or GitHub Discussions is enough. Every proposal, objection, and outcome gets logged.
  3. Pilot the community budgetA token amount (€150–300/month) that members allocate to events and projects. Small money, real power.
  4. Schedule the first quarterly State of dyadFounders share strategic decisions and their reasoning. Transparency without a vote.
